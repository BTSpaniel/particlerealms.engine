Under the hood

Pronunciation tools, careful measurements, and deeper listening.

Why words, sound pairs, and sentences?

Speech assessments use single words and connected speech because a sound can be clear on its own and harder to understand in a sentence. They also check sounds at the beginning, middle, and end of words. Listening tasks can offer choices or ask the listener to supply what they heard.

These short, original activities adapt those formats for listening to Navi. Word and pair scores measure choices; sentence scores count word edits, including extra and missing words. They do not score a child, diagnose a speech disorder, or measure individual phonemes directly.

For focused practice, start with sound pairs, check familiar words, then try sentences. A correct word does not establish that the same sound is clear in connected speech. Letter names are a separate exercise. Repeat missed targets when needed; progression is your choice, not an automatic clinical score.

Research on variability in children’s speech intelligibility scores found that listeners could improve on repeated presentations of the same speech. Our repeat reports compare the same targets and identify unchanged generated audio when available; practice gains alone do not prove an acoustic repair.

Sources: ASHA: speech sound assessment and ASHA: children’s speech perception assessment.
